Well control theory has a habit of pairing two ideas that sound almost identical but point in opposite directions — a leading indicator versus a lagging one, a barrier that's independent versus one that isn't. Get the pairing backwards and the reasoning that follows falls apart. Here are the fundamentals that trip people up most often, with the "why" behind each one, not just the answer.

## 1. What happens to pit level circulating a gas kick out

Circulating a gas kick out with the Driller's Method, active pit level increases as gas expands on its way up, then **decreases as that gas exits the choke** — not stays constant. Gas expanding in the annulus pushes pit level up, but once that same gas volume actually leaves the system through the choke, the level has to come back down to reflect what's gone. Treating "expanding" and "still in the system" as the same thing is the usual slip.

## 2. Leading indicator vs. lagging indicator of formation pressure

Change in ROP is a *leading* indicator of rising formation pressure — a drilling break shows up immediately as the bit cuts into a higher-pressure zone. **Change in background gas is the lagging one**: gas cut into the mud downhole doesn't show up at surface until it's actually circulated up, which takes real time. Mixing up "immediate" and "delayed" indicators is one of the most common conceptual slips in well control training.

## 3. Shale cutting density in a transition zone

Drilling into an abnormally pressured formation through a transition zone, shale cuttings come off the shakers **less dense, not more**. As pore pressure rises and the differential between mud weight and formation pressure narrows, cuttings arrive less compacted — a genuine, if subtle, warning sign that's easy to read backwards if you assume "more pressure" means "denser everything."

## 4. Defining kick tolerance correctly

Kick tolerance is **the maximum gas kick volume**, at a given kick intensity and depth, that can be shut in and circulated out without exceeding the well's weak-point fracture pressure — not the minimum kick intensity for a given volume. The two candidate definitions swap which variable is fixed and which is the limit being solved for. Worth reading kick-tolerance definitions twice before answering.

## 5. The "closing ratio" of a ram preventer

Closing ratio is a **mechanical ratio — packer area against piston rod area** — not a pressure figure. It's easy to reach for "the operating pressure needed to close against wellbore pressure" instead, since that's the practical thing crews actually care about, but that's a different spec entirely.

## 6. What actually causes an influx of a shallow hazard

Not filling the hole properly while tripping out is the real risk — **not pumping out of the hole**, which is itself a hazard-mitigation practice on some rigs, not a cause. Shallow hazards get triggered by the well going underbalanced, and an unfilled hole while pulling pipe is the classic way that happens fast.

## 7. Warning indicators of increasing formation pressure

When a question lists several valid warning signs (ROP change, gas readings, shale density, and others) and asks which one warns of increasing formation pressure, the answer is often **all of the above** — these indicators work together, and picking just one of several correct options is a common way to lose an otherwise easy question.

## 8. Operating practice in a gas-bearing top-hole section

In a top-hole section with a risk of gas-bearing formations, the right practice is to **control the rate of penetration**, not maintain a high one. Drilling fast into a shallow gas zone before you can read the warning signs is exactly the scenario that practice exists to prevent.

## 9. What actually governs gas blow-through at the shaker

The dimension that affects the pressure at which gas can blow through to the shaker area is the **height of the liquid seal**, not the body height or ID. The liquid seal is what's actually holding gas pressure back at that point in the system — the body dimensions matter for other things, but not this.

## 10. The most common cause of abnormally high formation pressure

Worldwide, the most common cause of abnormal pressure is **trapped fluid in shale** — not limestone fractures, which is a more dramatic-sounding but far less frequent cause. Compacting shale that can't expel its pore fluid fast enough is the mechanism behind the overwhelming majority of abnormally pressured zones.

## 11. How abnormal pressure affects primary well control

Drilling into an abnormally pressured formation **reduces** the hydrostatic overbalance your mud weight was providing — it doesn't increase it. The formation pressure has risen closer to (or past) your mud's hydrostatic pressure, which is precisely why abnormal pressure zones are dangerous.

## 12. A steady pit level isn't automatically good news

Drilling ahead with no change in pit level for an extended period doesn't mean nothing is happening — it can mean you're **gaining formation fluid** at a rate that hasn't shown up yet, not losing mud to the formation. A perfectly flat pit level is a checkpoint to interpret carefully, not a sign to relax.

## 13. Maximum Anticipated Surface Pressure vs. MAASP

The highest pressure you could see at surface if the well were completely full of formation fluid has its own specific term: **Maximum Anticipated Surface Pressure (MASP)** — not Maximum Allowable Annular Surface Pressure, which is a different, related-sounding acronym (MAASP) for a different concept (the surface pressure limit before you'd break down the weak point). The two get swapped constantly because they share half their letters.

## 14. Monitoring for BOP leaks

The safest, most reliable way to monitor for leaks through a closed BOP is lining up the **trip tank** to watch for flow above it — not just watching the flow rate indicator, which is a coarser, less sensitive check. The trip tank catches small volumes that a flow indicator alone can miss.

## 15. Pit level while displacing cement with mud

Once cement has been pumped inside the casing and mud is displacing it into position, active pit level should **stay constant** — it shouldn't be increasing. Anything else at that stage is a sign something's going wrong, not a normal part of the job.

## 16. Cuttings in the annulus and bottom hole pressure

Cuttings loaded into the annulus **increase** bottom hole pressure — they add density to that column, they don't reduce it. It's a small effect but a real one, and assuming cuttings "dilute" the annulus rather than adding to it is the wrong instinct.

## 17. Accumulator pressure that won't recover after closing an annular

If accumulator pressure drops when closing an annular preventer, the BOP closes fine, and there are no visible leaks, the likely cause is that the **accumulator charge pump hasn't started up** to bring pressure back — not a stuck 3-position valve, which would usually show up as a different symptom (the BOP failing to close properly in the first place).

## 18. Barrier independence

An annular and a ram on the same BOP stack are **not independent barriers** of each other — they share the same stack, the same hydraulic system in many cases, and common failure modes. Kill weight mud in the well and the BOP, by contrast, genuinely are independent of one another, which is the pairing that's easy to get backwards.

## 19. A big ROP jump on a repeat bit run is a flow-check moment

Coming back to bottom with the same bit and same weight-on-bit, but seeing ROP triple compared to the earlier run, is worth stopping for. The right move is a **flow check, with circulating bottoms-up as a follow-on consideration** — not jumping straight to shutting in, which skips the step that would actually tell you whether you have a problem.

## 20. Tour change handovers during a well kill

Mid-kill, a tour change isn't the moment to move fast. Best practice is for **outgoing and incoming crew to overlap long enough for a full handover** — not for the new crew to take over immediately and sort out the details with the supervisor as they go. A kill in progress is exactly when a rushed handover costs the most.

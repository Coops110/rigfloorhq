Safety boots are the one piece of PPE every person on a rig buys for themselves, wears for twelve hours straight, and replaces more often than anything else. They are also the piece most often bought on price or looks alone. This guide covers what rig work actually asks of a boot, how to read the certification label, and the trade-offs between the main choices. The wider PPE picture — hard hats, FR clothing, hand protection — is in the [rig floor PPE guide](/blog/rig-floor-ppe-guide).

## Key Takeaways

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What is the minimum standard? | ASTM F2413 in North America; EN ISO 20345, usually S3, in the UK, Europe, the Middle East and much of Africa. |
| Steel or composite toe? | Either. Both pass the same impact and compression tests. Composite is lighter and does not conduct cold. |
| Do I need metatarsal guards? | If your job involves handling tubulars or working under suspended loads, often yes — and some operators require them. |
| Pull-on or lace-up? | Pull-ons for quick on/off and no snagging laces; lace-ups for ankle support when climbing. |
| What else matters most on a rig? | An oil-resistant, slip-resistant outsole. Drilling fluid on steel decks is one of the slipperiest surfaces you will work on. |
| When do I replace them? | After any heavy hit to the toe cap, or when the tread is worn smooth or the sole is separating. |

## What Rig Work Asks of a Boot

A rig floor puts several different hazards under your feet at once, and a boot that covers one of them well can still fail at another:

- **Dropped and swinging iron.** Tongs, slips, chain, subs and pipe ends. This is what the safety toe and metatarsal guard are for.
- **Wet, oily steel.** Drilling fluid, hydraulic oil and rain on steel plate. This is the outsole's job, and it is where cheap boots fall down first.
- **Oil and chemicals.** Oil-based mud and hydrocarbons soften ordinary rubber soles until they lose grip and fall apart.
- **Long hours on hard decks.** A twelve-hour tour on steel is hard on feet, knees and backs. Comfort is not a luxury here — a boot that hurts gets loosened, swapped or worn badly.
- **Stairs, ladders and the monkeyboard.** Climbing needs a defined heel and decent ankle support.
- **Electrical equipment.** Generator skids, the SCR or VFD house, and top drive connections, where an electrical hazard rated sole can be required.

## Reading the Label: ASTM F2413

In the US and Canada, safety footwear is certified to **ASTM F2413**. A compliant boot carries a label inside the tongue or shaft listing the standard and edition year, then codes for what it has been tested to protect against:

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **I** | Impact-resistant toe cap |
| **C** | Compression-resistant toe cap |
| **Mt** | Metatarsal protection |
| **EH** | Electrical hazard — the sole insulates against accidental contact with live circuits, in dry conditions |
| **PR** | Puncture-resistant sole plate |
| **SD** | Static dissipative |
| **Cd** | Conductive |

A rig boot should show **I** and **C** at the very least. Look for **Mt** if your job involves handling tubulars, and **EH** if you work around electrical equipment. Do not assume anything that is not on the label: a boot sold as "work boot" or "safety style" with no F2413 label is not a safety boot.

## Reading the Label: EN ISO 20345

The UK, Europe, the Middle East and much of Africa use **EN ISO 20345**. Its ratings are grouped, and **S3** is the usual requirement for rig work. An S3 boot has a 200-joule toe cap, a closed heel, antistatic properties, an energy-absorbing heel, a water-resistant upper, a puncture-resistant midsole and a cleated outsole.

Extra markings worth looking for on a rig boot:

- **SR** (current edition) or **SRC** (older edition) — tested for slip resistance.
- **HRO** — heat-resistant outsole, useful around hot work.
- **M** — metatarsal protection.

If you work internationally, check which standard your operator specifies. Many boots are certified to both.

## Steel vs. Composite vs. Alloy Toe

| | Steel | Composite | Alloy (aluminium, titanium) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protection | Meets the standard | Meets the standard | Meets the standard |
| Weight | Heaviest | Lightest | Between the two |
| Cold and heat | Conducts both | Insulates | Conducts both |
| Toe bulk | Thinnest cap, slimmest toe | Thicker cap, roomier toe box | Thin cap |
| Metal detectors | Sets them off | Does not | Sets them off |

All three give the same rated protection, because they have to pass the same tests. The choice comes down to weight, climate and fit. Composite suits cold locations — the North Sea, Canada in winter — and anyone who wants a lighter boot for long tours. Steel is often cheaper and gives a slimmer profile.

## Metatarsal Guards

A toe cap protects the toes only. A pipe end, a dropped slip or a tong jaw landing on the top of the foot hits the metatarsal bones behind the cap, which the cap does nothing to protect. A **metatarsal guard** covers that area.

Guards are either **internal**, built into the boot under the upper, or **external**, a hard flap over the laces. Internal guards are lighter and do not catch on anything. External guards give more coverage and are easy to see during a PPE check. The PPE guide notes that met guards are [increasingly specified for floor hands and derrickmen](/blog/rig-floor-ppe-guide) handling tubulars, and some operators now require them for any job on the floor.

## Pull-On vs. Lace-Up

**Pull-on boots** — wellington or roper style — are very common on rigs. They go on and off in seconds at crew change, there are no laces to snag on equipment or soak up mud, and if drilling fluid or a chemical gets inside, they can be kicked off fast. The drawbacks are a looser fit around the ankle and heel, and less support when climbing.

**Lace-up boots** hold the foot and ankle firmly, which matters on stairs, ladders, uneven decks and the monkeyboard. They take longer to get on and off, and the laces need tucking away or replacing when they get oil-soaked.

A common split is pull-ons for floor hands and lace-ups for derrickmen and anyone climbing most of the tour. If you choose lace-ups, a side zip or speed lacing makes crew change much easier.

## The Outsole Matters Most

The toe cap protects you in an incident. The outsole protects you every step of every tour. On a rig, look for a sole that is:

- **Oil-resistant**, usually nitrile rubber. Ordinary rubber swells and softens in oil-based mud and hydrocarbons.
- **Slip-resistant**, with a tested rating, not just a "slip-resistant" claim on the box.
- **Well cleated but not packed tight.** Deep, open lugs shed mud. Tight, narrow tread clogs and turns into a smooth surface.
- **Heat-resistant** if you do or work near hot work.

Slips and trips are one of the most common causes of lost-time injuries offshore, which is why traction and housekeeping around the floor matter as much as the boot itself.

## Comfort, Fit and Climate

- **Try them on at the end of the day**, when your feet are largest, with the socks you will actually work in.
- **Leave room at the toe.** Your toes should not touch the cap when you walk, or the cap will bruise them on every step.
- **Waterproof membranes** keep rain and wash-down water out, but also hold sweat in. They suit cold, wet locations more than hot ones.
- **Insulation** matters for cold-weather and North Sea work. For the Middle East or West Africa, breathable and uninsulated is the better choice.
- **Removable insoles** let you add a cushioned or supportive insole for long tours on steel.

## When to Replace Your Boots

Replace your boots straight away if:

- The toe cap has taken a **heavy impact or crush**, even if it looks fine. The cap may no longer meet its rating.
- The **cap is exposed**, or the upper is cut through near it.
- The **sole is separating** from the upper.
- The **tread is worn smooth**, especially at the heel and ball of the foot.
- **Oil has softened the sole**, so it feels spongy or sticky.

Boots on rig work rarely last more than a year of regular tours, and often less. Budget for them as a regular expense, not a once-in-a-while purchase.

## Check Your Company's PPE Policy First

Everything above is general guidance. The operator's or drilling contractor's PPE policy decides what is actually required on your location — including the standard, whether metatarsal guards or EH soles are mandatory, and sometimes whether pull-ons are allowed at all. Check it before you buy, especially before your first hitch on a new rig. If you are starting out, the [roughneck career guide](/careers/roughneck) covers what else to expect.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What boots are required on an oil rig?

At minimum, a safety-toe boot certified to ASTM F2413 in North America or EN ISO 20345 (S3 is the usual rig requirement) in the UK, Europe, the Middle East and much of Africa. Many operators also require oil-resistant, slip-resistant outsoles, and some specify metatarsal guards or electrical hazard soles for particular jobs. The operator's or drilling contractor's PPE policy is the final word.

### Are composite toe boots allowed on a rig?

Yes. A composite toe certified to ASTM F2413 passes the same impact and compression tests as a steel toe. Composite is lighter, does not conduct heat or cold, and contains no metal, which suits cold climates and electrical work. Steel caps are thinner, so a steel-toe boot can have a slightly less bulky toe box.

### Should I get pull-on or lace-up boots for rig work?

Both are common. Pull-on boots go on and off quickly, have no laces to snag or soak up mud and oil, and can be kicked off fast if chemicals get inside. Lace-ups hold the ankle more firmly, which helps on stairs, ladders and uneven decks. Many floor hands wear pull-ons; derrickmen and anyone climbing a lot often prefer lace-ups.

### When should I replace my safety boots?

Replace them after any heavy impact or crush on the toe cap, even if it looks undamaged, because the cap may no longer meet its rating. Also replace them when the tread is worn smooth, the sole is separating, the upper is cut through to the cap, or oil has softened the sole.
